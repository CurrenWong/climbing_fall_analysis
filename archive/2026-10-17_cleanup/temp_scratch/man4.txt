 15936| Most of the contact algorithms implemented in FEBio offer two contact enforcement methods:
 15937| a penalty method and an augmented Lagrangian method. Since the strategies for these two
 15938| methods are slightly different, we will look at them separately. These algorithms are discussed in
 15939| much more detail in the FEBio Theory Manual.
 15940| 8.5.1 The penalty method
 15941| The penalty method enforces contact by “penalizing” (in an energy sense) any deviation from
 15942| the contact constraint. In other words, when the two contacting surfaces penetrate, a force is
 15943| generated proportional to the distance of penetration, which has the effect that the surfaces will
 15944| now repel each other. The strength of this repelling force is controlled by the user through the
 15945| penalty factor.
 15946| The obvious downside of this method is that some penetration is necessary to generate the
 15947| contact force. The larger the penalty factor, the smaller this penetration needs to be and thus
 15948| the better the contact constraint is enforced. However, choosing the penalty factor too large may
 15949| cause the problem to become unstable since the contact force (and corresponding stiffness) will
 15950| dominate the other forces in the model. Choosing a good penalty factor can thus be often difficult
 15951| and may require several attempts before getting it “right”. To help with choosing a penalty factor,
 15952| FEBio offers the auto_penalty option for many of its contact interfaces which will estimate a good
 15953| penalty factor based on element size and initial material stiffness. However, even then it may still
 15954| take a few tries before getting the penalty factor good enough.
 15955| 8.5.2 Augmented Lagrangian Method
 15956| In theory, in the presence of constraints, the corresponding Lagrange multipliers must be cal-
 15957| culated which, in the case of contact, correspond to the contact forces that enforce the contact
 15958| constraint exactly. Unfortunately, the exact evaluation of these Lagrange multipliers is numerically
 15959| very challenging and therefore in FEBio it was decided to evaluate these Lagrange multipliers us-
 15960| inganiterativemethod,namedtheAugmentedLagrangianmethod. Usingthismethod,FEBiowill
 15961| solve a time step several times (these iterations are termed augmentations), where each time the
 15962| approximate Lagrange multipliers are updated (or augmented).
 15963| The obvious drawback of this method is that now each time step has to be solved several
 15964| times. However,theadvantageofavoidingthenumericalproblemsofobtainingtheexactLagrange
 15965| multipliersandthefactthatinmostcontactproblemsverylittleextraworkisneededtosolvethese
 15966| augmentations,makesthismethodveryattractive. Inaddition,itoftengivesbetterenforcementof
 15967| the contact constraint compared to the penalty method.
 15968| So why not just use the augmented Lagrangian method? Well, often the penalty method will
 15969| give good results and since the penalty method is much faster, it is often the preferred choice of
 15970| many analysts.

 15979| 8.5.3 Initial Separation
 15980| FEBiooftenstruggleswithproblemsthathaveaninitialseparation. Thisisespeciallysowhenthe
 15981| problem is force-driven, meaning that the deformation of the model is driven by a force (opposed
 15982| to displacement-driven problems). In general, when the contact interface is necessary to define

 15986| 8.6 Cautionary Note for Steady-State Biphasic and Multiphasic Anal-
 15987| yses
 15988| Biphasic, biphasic-solute and multiphasic analyses are generally transient analyses, involving
 15989| mass transport (solvent and solutes) through a porous permeable domain. The default setting
 15990| for these analyses is the transient mode (see Section 3.2). When a steady-state analysis is re-
 15991| quested (<analysis type="steady-state"/>), the code simplifies the governing equations by setting
 15992| time derivatives of the solid displacement and solute concentrations to zero. It is important to un-
 15993| derstand that this simplification is only applicable to analyses where interface conditions between
 15994| sub-domains, and boundary conditions with the external environment, allow mass exchanges for
 15995| the solvent and all solutes. If a domain or sub-domain is completely sealed such that it prevents
 15996| solvent or solute exchanges, a steady-state analysis should not be used, as it would not capture
 15997| thesesealedconditionsandwouldleadtoerroneousresults. Forthosetypesofproblems,always
 15998| use a transient analysis to obtain the correct solution. In those cases, a steady-state response
 15999| may be obtained efficiently by temporarily increasing the transport properties (hydraulic perme-
 16000| ability and solute diffusivities) by orders of magnitude via load curves, and/or by increasing the
 16001| size of time steps.
